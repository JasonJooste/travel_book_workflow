I met Adrien at the sparse and dusty border between Nigeria and Cameroon. I was sitting on the stairs, hungry, peeling an orange. I was grumpy. I'd fallen in the slippery bulldust and scraped my knee. It was mid-afternoon and I hadn't eaten all day, had only one orange left and no cash to buy more food. There was no atm and the next town was 30km away. I was going to have to ride hungry.

The border guard had also just casually informed me that the border was in fact closed and that I'd have to travel 500km to Yaoundé for my stamp and visa. Adrien rolled in to the border post on his slick gravel bike, also covered in a thick layer of fine dust. Being a native French speaker, he had a much longer discussion with the official. For a moment, I was hopeful. Perhaps the Romand would be able to negotiate a solution that didn't involve illegally riding for a week to the capital. 

No luck. Once again, Africa resisted the longing for certainty and control of its uptight western visitors. 

We chatted briefly. He was currently attempting the record for the fastest traversal of Africa from northernmost Tunisa to southernmost South Africa. He was averaging 12 hours and over 200km per day and had covered in two months what had taken me over a year. Both seasoned travellers, we recognised a lost cause and quickly accepted our coming illegal stint. He politely offered for me to join him and I politely declined in horror. Then, he raced off into the distance, leaving behind a literal trail of dust and me with my grumbling stomach. And then, in my hunger, I promptly forgot about him. 

The next I heard of Adrien, he'd been missing for nine days. At my comparatively plodding pace, I'd reached Yaoundé around 10 days after crossing the border and then spent a few days there getting things repaired and recovering from a bout of malaria. I'd visited the General Delegation for National Security, the office mentioned by the border guard, that afternoon and was told to go to the airport for my stamp and visa. There I met an English woman, who lived in Limbé. My plan was to head for Limbé the next morning, so we exchanged contacts. But first I would visit the airport to finally get my documents in order. Fate had other plans. 

She texted me out of the blue that evening asking if I knew this guy Adrien. His GPS tracker, which was published online for his record attempt, had paused for four hours just north of Yaoundé before racing south at over 80km/h and finally stopping in the ministry of defence. News was slowly filtering through from the cycling community to the Cameroonian expat community and people were worried. The expats were understandably wary of anything to do with the military and were hesitant to intervene. As a scruffy cyclotourist, I had less to lose and was, perhaps naively, happy to try and lend a hand.  

Two things might have happened to Adrien, both bad. Either he'd crashed and been rushed to the public military hospital situated in the ministry of defence compound and wasn't replying because his phone was smashed or, God forbid, he was in a coma. Or, he'd been taken by the military and was in jail, without his phone. These options called for pretty different approaches.  

The plan was thus: I would stop by the military hospital on the way to the airport. It was directly on the way and I wanted urgently to see how he was doing. An older German cyclist had recently died alone in a Nouakchott hospital after a traffic accident and I was having visions of Adrien alone and unconscious, with nobody to manage his care. If he wasn't there I would get my visa in order before returning to ask around in the ministry. Dealing with the military is fraught at the best of times. Worse still, if he'd truly been arrested for not having a visa, turning up without one myself would be at best cheeky and at worst recklessly daring. {{awkard phrasing and tenses. What can I do??}}

Of course, nothing went to plan. The next morning, I arrived sweating at the hospital and parked my bike, cutting my way through the humid tropical air. There was no central reception, just open corridors humming with haphazard air-conditioners, their drips feeding rivulets on the concrete floors. I hunted through the various departments, my task complicated by my barely-servicable French and the fact that a Swiss doctor had just started volunteering there and everybody thought I was looking for him. After searching through all the departments, including the VIP area of the hospital, I bumped into a volunteering French doctor who took me under her wing. The most efficient woman I think I've ever met, within minutes she'd sat me down, reassured me, understood the problem and called a nurse to escourt me. I managed to hold myself back from accidentally calling her mum.

Adrien wasn't in the hospital, but my guardian nurse said that he might be in an infirmary on the other side of the ministry campus. A soldier wanted to know where we were going. I showed him a photo, stretching the truth only a little by saying I was looking for a friend. 

"Oh, I know where he is", he responded with a big smile. "Come with me"

The first snowball of this tropical avalanche had started to roll. I still hoped we were heading to a medical facility, but my gut told me otherwise. We passed through a checkpost - SEMIL - sécurité militaire - military security. Option 2. Well, guess who's now a cheeky, reckless, daring boy? They took my phone and passport. Straight away an officer stopped us, furious. 

... How could I disrespect them by entering the area in shorts? Whew. I made my deepest apologies. Luckily, I'd packed my embassy pants and the guard led me through a gate to quickly drop my pants and change in the overgrown garden of a building, hoping desperately that I wasn't mooning a general. With knees now deferentially covered, I was led into a courtyard and to a concrete façade punctured with prison bars. A waxy, sallow face regarded me from behind the bars. There he was. 

Too caught up the sudden victory and his needs to worry too much about my situation, I talked to an extremely surprised Adrien. The random cyclist from the border was the last person he expected to see while in military detention. He shuffled quickly to the bars to speak with me. They yelled at him to step back. Then, even though he'd been detained for a week and half without any outside contact, they brought him out and plopped him on a plastic chair next to me. They forced us to speak in French so they could understand. I got straight to the point. Was there anyone he wanted me to contact? Any special message he wanted me to send? He was clearly weak, but remarkably composed. He gave me contact details for his dad and girlfriend, just to let them know he was okay. He also asked me to contact the Swiss consulate. 

As I was writing this all down, the atmosphere grew suddenly grew palpably more tense and an officer dressed all in white strode in, black shoes and sunglasses gleaming. Livid, he marched me into a dim and sat me down on a bench opposite two sauve-looking men in grey suits. I'm cooked, I thought. No visa. About to be interrogated by military police. I knew I'd be released eventually, but it could take weeks. In Mauritania, I'd already learnt how little sway the Australian government had in Africa. Not to mention the embarrassment. Entering the bloody ministry of defence without a valid visa, searching for someone who'd been detained for not having a bloody visa. People don't call that daring. They call that stupid.  

They let me sweat. For five minutes they chatted casually amongst themselves. Eventually, their heads turned lazily in my direction.

"What do you think of Cameroon", they asked me, politely, in French. 

"Lovely country. The infrastructure has been great" I responded, a psychotically desperate piece of me fleetingly hoping they'd release me in a burst of pure, nationalistic pride. No luck.

"Have you encountered any corruption on the way? Anything out of the ordinary?" An escalation. 

"Not at all. Everyone has been polite and efficient." which was, fortunately, mostly true.

"You're also a cyclist? How many kilometres have you travelled??"

I started to relax a little. Apart from deliberately ignoring me this was starting to feel like the travel conversation that I'd had 1000 times before. Home turf. Maybe that was the point, because then, suddenly

"How about visas. Do you have a valid visa?" Shit. 

I briefly considered an outright lie, but settled for a lightly-massaged truth. 

"I have an e-visa but the border post didn't have a stamp. They told me to come to Yaoundé to get my stamp sorted". Nothing illegal here. Just a regrettable office-supply mishap.

Before they had a chance to dig further, Adrien was escourted into the room. Their heads swivelled to him and, barely seated, his story story spewed forth. This was the first time in 10 days he'd had the chance to speak to anyone important. 

He had been racing on the highway south to Yaoundé. At dusk, he took a picture of the sun setting over a bridge. Although seemingly benign, this bridge was in fact part of a huge new hydroelectric dam, the pride of Cameroonian government, which was about to supply 30% of the country's electricity. The soldiers guarding the dam were understandably a little on edge, though of course, anyone with a phone and a car could have filmed the whole thing 100 times over. They stopped him and told him to delete the photo. So far, quite a common occurrence in Central Africa.

Unfortunately, and I'm not sure if this is a factor in spy school, but Adrien *looks* like he could be a spy. He had a neat haircut, a clean-shaven, chisled jaw and a pristine moustache, despite his hours on the road. Compare and contrast to the author's ragged, sweaty melon and haystack hair. Perhaps as a consequence, they asked for his passport. Things devolved from there. 

Obviously, they didn't find his entry stamp. After a few hours of argument he was whisked away to the ministry of defence for processing. The cherry on top was when they asked him about his work before professional cycling, to which he'd unthinkingly responded that he'd been a police officer. 

The witness ended his testimony and began his impromptu legal defence. "I'm in the same position as him!", he said, shocking me to full attention.

I almost kicked him in the shins. He was too busy defending himself for any frantic eyebrow waggling. All I could do was keep my mouth shut. Luckily, the conversation breezed past this interjection and we moved on to his lack of medical care. The men in suits agreed for him to visit the hospital and get his phone the following day. Business closed. Adrien was taken back to his cell and, to my immense relief, I was allowed to leave. 

I immediately went to a bar and got myself a beer to calm my nerves and start contacting people. High on adrenaline and relief, I expected a sort of heros welcome from his family. Things were a little more complicated than that. In retrospect very understandably, Adrien's father was at first very suspicious. He had been contacted by an Australian number in English, supposedly from Cameroon. I learnt later that the family had already interacted with a few guys, one of them even in distant Senegal, claiming that they knew where Adrien was and could get him released. He, sensibly, only started to trust me after a few days. Adrien's girlfriend, on the other hand, was much more receptive. She was frustrated with the lack of information and progress from the Swiss consulate and was deeply relieved to know that he was alive and at least mentally well. We made a plan for me to deliver him a message from her the next day.

A heavy afternoon storm rolled through as I had finally gotten my visa from the airport, luckily an easy process. I sat under cover, processing my relief and forging a plan for the next days while absent-mindedly fending off questions from two mechanics who wanted to know where to find white girls to date. The plan: I would print out the letter the next morning and try to deliver it to Adrien. I was less worried, now that I had my visa, but I was still unsure whether they would be upset after the hassle I'd caused the day before. If I'd embarrassed anyone by walking straight in like that. If I wouldn't be welcome at all. If I made it in, after the visit I would talk to the Swiss consulate. I was worried if I visited them sooner they would tell me to mind my own business and Adrien might never get the letter. 

The next morning, I was welcomed with smiles in the compound. They showed me to the cell and this time we were allowed to talk for longer. He'd received medical care (but not his phone) that morning and was already looking a lot brighter. He read the letter from his girlfriend and thanked me. Honestly, there wasn't much to do past this point. After covering the essentials, talk drifted to cycling. After half an hour we were almost chatting like normal travellers, about bikes and parts and sights we'd seen. After another half an hour, conversation slowed. I wanted to stay, both as support and distraction, but there wasn't much left to say. I said goodbye and that I'd come back tomorrow. 

From there I went directly to the embassy. The consul, Rolf, took me to his office and offered me the best coffee I'd had in a while, with creamy fresh milk. Sipping indulgently, I learnt my worries were unfounded. He was curious, engaged and thankful for the information. The embassy had been working hard in the background trying to see Adrien, but had been blocked by the military at every pass - a violation of the Vienna Convention. He was very glad to know Adrien's exact location and that he was being cared for - news he'd received from the military but wasn't sure it was accurate. Things were so bad that he was considering just turning up at the ministry himself, we even considered going together the next day. He gave me his personal contact details to pass on to Adrien's loved ones.

Thursday and Friday passed without much to report. Adrien had been permanently moved to a VIP room in the hospital with an aircoditioner and a personal guard. I exchanged letters between him and his girlfriend and brought him a book and some chocolate, neither of which he needed in the end. The guard had brought his Playstation and they were playing video games and watching movies together. I naively imagined the handover would be short and perfunctory. Sweet summer child. The guests filtered in one by one. As if their breaking of the Vienna convention was some routine box-checking exercise. More pleasantries. Adrien is an elite endurance athlete with unbreakable willpower. Literally the day after his release, he was back on his bike, riding back to the bridge where he was taken. A casual 100km.
