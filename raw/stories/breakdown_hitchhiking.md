I left Tolouse on the 30th - Halloween - the mighty Pyranees looming between me and Barcelona. The Pas de Peyrol was peanuts compared to this. My first big mountain range. 

The climbing started slowly and steadily. I covered 120km the first day, despite the incline {{true??}}. I'd already lost about 10kg and my body was slowly adapting to the daily strain. I was starting to feel strong. The pedals responding smoothly to the force of my legs. 

I rolled into a small tourist town as the sun was setting. I hunted for a place to stay, but all the guest houses were fully booked and I was ravenous. Focusing on the short term, I found a Burger King and ate a far-too-expensive veggie burger, watching the families with dressed-up kids, and groups of teenagers around me, ready to go out {{something funny comparing young kids to old kids dessing up}}. I contacted a Warmshowers host last minute, asking if I could stay the night. She responded icily that it was too late. Feeling homesick and lonely, I had no choice but to continue. 

Stomach full and limbs heavy, I crawled uphill in the dark, searching for a place to pitch my tent in the sprawling tourist town. My light died. I pulled into a construction site on my left. There was a building, no door and cement bags lying around. I dragged my bike up the stairs and into the house, laying my sleeping bag on the floor, tucked behind a wall. My tent would have been too visible. My first night in an abandoned building. 

Luckily, my exhaustion was more powerful than the stress of the uncertain surroundings. If someone was going to murder me it would have been a little on the nose for Halloween night. Some drunk teenagers walked past the house, yelling and throwing bottles {{did they?}}. The small spike of adrenaline faded as they stalked into the distance and I slowly drifted off to sleep, wondering whether my friends and I had spiked anyone's adrenaline in the bad old days. {{elaborate??}}

I realised the next morning that, after 6 weeks on the road {{true??}}, I finally knew where all of my luggage belonged. I could pack up my stuff blindfolded. Hopefully not necessary, but it was another sign that my body and mind were slowly adapting to my new life on the road. 

Both were tested and one fell short. The next day was a climb without end. Winding up the hills, cars zipping past, my motivation started to wane. A foot bath in some hot springs temporarily raised my spirits, which subsequently dropped in proportion to my rising elevation. Evidently, my body hadn't adapted as much as I'd thought. I suppose hours of endless heavily-laden climbing will do that to you. I pulled over into a field, laid out my ground sheet and had a picnic. Which turned into a nap. Which turned into scrolling on my phone, installing tinder and despair. By the time I escaped the hole, it was 5:30pm and too late to continue. I set up my tent. My demons clearly don't skip leg day and can follow me up mountains. 

The next day was straight-up brutality. A single, unending climb of 1200m, with an average gradient of 4% {{true?}}. It just didn't stop and neither did I. Neither did the traffic. This was the main road to the tiny country of Andorra, perched on the mountains between Spain and France. I briefly considered a detour to cross the border, but my legs were already cooked and I'm not really into stamp collecting. 

At altitude, an annoyance poked its head in the fresh mountain air. Stopping even briefly sucked all the heat from my body, so even for a brief stop, I needed to stack on layers. When, I moved again, the layers came off slowly. Descents were a shiver-fest. Ascending, I'd have rather been naked {{funny}}. A level of logistics that I thought I'd left well behind me on my world tour.
