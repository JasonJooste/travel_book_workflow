I've never been particularly careful with what I eat and drink. You'd think I'd have learnt my lesson after a month of giardia in Pakistan, but if you want to keep your fix of schadenfreude you'd best leave the thinking to me. The International Overlanders' Society mandates at least one bout of diarrhoea per quarter and I wanted to get mine in early. I didn't need much encouragement. The mountain stream was ice cold and crystal clear. Surely fresh and bitingly crisp. 

I was on a high after crossing the brutal Pas de Peyrol. The last few kilometres have a gradient of over 10%. I emerged through a thick fog at the summit, triumphant, dripping a mix of condensation and sweat, welcomed by the warm lights of a tiny tourist café were barely visible through the fog. Inside, the staff and customers were delightfully French and ignored me studiously, giving the impression they'd been quite enjoying the bad weather and the lack of paying customers. I celebrated my conquest by quietly dripping in the corner with a cold sandwich and a beer.

A magnificent descent followed, smooth and perfectly surfaced through the golden autumn forest. I gleamed with sweat and joy in the dying light. Leaving myself a sweet little descent for breakfast, I pulled over halfway down the mountain. There was a beautiful spot next to a stream, with a perfect, flat surface covered in autumn leaves for my tent. 

After such a long day, usually I'd be lazy. I'd cook dinner, hang my clothes to dry and pass out. But, I was dangerously motivated and jumped into the ice cold stream, nipples cutting sharply through the fog. Entranced by the struggle and triumph of the day, the beauty of the surroundings and probably still in shock from the cold, I ignored my newly-purchased water filter and drank the mountain water. 

The next morning, I was inexplicably tired. I had also run out of food. It took me until 9am, well after my planned 7am start, to finally eat my breakfast of oats, tomato sauce and margarine. The fever hit as I was packing up my things. Without food, there was no way I could stay, so I cruised downhill, letting the fog and wind cool my aching body. When the slope flattened and the strain on my legs increased, things got more difficult. After half an hour of riding, I lay down in the sun and passed out for 15 minutes, shivering. 

Usually, this is the point where I'd find a guest house to shiver in and regret my dietary choices, but I'd agreed to be hosted by a friend-of-a-friend that evening and had another 60km to go. I was clearly sick, but the distance was short by normal standards and the draw of a friendly face and a warm bed was strong. If I was very lucky, maybe even some hot soup. So, waking up from my fretful, early-morning nap, I decided to push on.

It was tough going. Every half an hour I needed to stop for a break. My legs were weak and shook when I stood up. The fog cleared and the usually-welcome sunlight of the cloudless day exacerbated my fever. I was hungry and stopped in a small village for a snack and yet another break.

The contents of my Woolies food bag was sadder than ever. A puddle of days-old, oily cheese, smelling much more Roqueforte than its original Cheddar, and a shaker of salt. I needed food and, suddenly, another helper appeared. I was sitting on a park bench, working up the energy to walk to the shop. A man approached me. Kind, blue, sympathetic eyes. Grey hair. Probably mid 60s, but it's hard to tell given how much the French smoke. He asked me if I needed a hand.

I was overconfident in my French. I'd read some books and watched some shows. I'd used Duolingo, an app for language learning, every day for a few months. I could barely speak. It didn't stop this kind stranger though, who offered to take me to buy some food. I dazedly accepted and hopped in his van.

I assumed we'd head down the road in the village. We didn't. We got on the main road in the direction I'd been riding, away from the village and BoB. After about 5 minutes I tried asking where we were going. I didn't have the words. So I continued south, in the white van of a strange man with whom I'd exchanged a few, broken sentences.

Now that I've got you nice and worried, just a tiny aside about risk. For those that have never travelled, it might seem strange to hop in the van of a stranger after two minutes of conversation. You'll see, however, that an important common theme in all these stories is that surrendering to the kindness of strangers is a calculated risk. Sometimes things can go wrong, but rarely catastrophically. Things can be awkward, for sure, as you will see. They can be physically uncomfortable. They can be unpredictable. But rarely do they end up life threatening.

And that's the difference. Many people, quite reasonably, draw the line at discomfort. When they're sick, they want a warm bed and good food. When they're in a new place, they take some time to orient themselves. For me and many others, it's a little different. I know that the best stories happen in uncertainty and chaos and have a rocky, possibly sadistic, relationship with my body and its comfort. So I set the bar somewhere else. For me, a day where I don't eat can be a good day and a good story. Where I'm harassed by police. Where I'm scammed. As long as I come out alive, healthy and not too traumatised, I'm happy. So it's worth keeping a question in mind when hearing travel stories: what's really at stake? It may be that you're setting the bar somewhere very different from the daring raconteur in front of you. This day, the bar was set so bloody high that this daring, raconteuring so-and-so was shivering in a stranger's car instead of a warm guest-house bed. 

In the van, I was starting to get uncomfortable. We'd been driving for 15 minutes and I had no idea where we were going. I tried to ask where. He didn't understand. Then, thankfully, we rolled up to the supermarket in the next village. He told me to pick whatever food I wanted so I got some bread and cheese. At the checkout, he insisted on paying for me.

In the car ride home I was both annoyed and relieved. This town was on the way for me and this little detour had cost me close to an hour on a big and difficult day of riding. It was trumped by the relief of still having all my organs. Obviously, he'd just taken me to the next town over for a better supermarket. The sudden drop in adrenaline revealed a fever that had been quietly stoking the boiler. I was still sick and very keen to get back to BoB. We drove back in silence. 

I unpacked my things for a small picnic. I'd been hungry when we'd almost an hour before. Usually, I'd be more sociable, but I was sick and hungry. I thanked our mystery helper and offered to take a photo with him for my collection. He refused, shyly, his kind eyes turning to the ground. After a lot of back and forth, it turned out he wasn't being humble. He was ashamed of his appearance. Of his wrinkles. 

As I busied myself, a little pointedly, with my bread and cheese, he stood and watched. Doubling down, he invited me to stay the night. I told him that my friends were waiting for me. He insisted that I was in no state to ride. Then it clicked - he was hitting on me. Damn it Jason, once again seduced with free food. I'm not sure what he was planning to do with an exhausted, sick boy with a fever that night. Maybe the shivering helps. He backed off awkwardly after I insisted for the fifth time.

As I set off through the green fields, I remembered being hit on by another middle-aged man who'd hit on me during a quick picnic near a block of public toilets a few days before. It seems like the gay scene was still very much underground in the French countryside. I rolled past the supermarket we'd visited, frustrated at how a single direct question could have avoided this hour-long delay. I also felt sorry for him. Kind eyes trained to peer warily out a foxhole. 

The fever got worse and the time between stops shortened. Beads of sweat turned to rivulets to streams. My fever rose. Soon the rests were longer than the rides. Eventually, I gave up. I texted my host, asking him to pick me up. I put on all of my clothes, with my black wet-weather gear on the outside and lay in the sun by the side of the road, shivering. My track pants were inside-out. I lay for an hour gathering the strength to change them. After three hours, my host arrived. He'd driven more than an hour to pick me up. We loaded my bike into the car and set of for his home. Thankfully, this car trip was a lot less awkward and the soup was delicious.
