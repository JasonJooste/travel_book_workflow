Alice and I decided to split up for a bit. I was itching to touch African soil and the endless rows of beach houses and apartments was grating on me. Each town exactly the same. She would take the bus for a section, giving me a chance to really put my foot down on the pedal, as it were. Did things go to plan? Are you even asking? 

On Saturday evening, she found out that all the tickets at reasonable hours were sold out, leaving only 6am. We set up camp in a olive field about 20km from the bus station, finding a flat spot between the dusty furrows. I slept under the stars. The beauty of the night kept me up. I watched the stars late into the night, despite the early start the next day. 

Up at 3am, we groggily packed our things and set off. By 5:45, Alice was safely on the bus and I was free to test my mettle. I realised too late that Alice had taken all the food with her. In Spain, especially in winter, the shops wouldn't be open for hours. I left Albacete anway, trusting in Fate's bounties on the route. 

That trust was mislaid. By midday, I'd covered more than 50km on the beautiful Don Quixote route - a gravel bike path following an old rail line. The beauty was unfortunately counterbalanced by my empty stomach. I hadn't eaten anything all day and had only a foraged pomegranate left in my bags. It was Sunday and all the villages were deserted. Bloody villagers enjoying their bloody lives with their bloody families. My powerbank was also empty, after a few nights of camping.

I searched a village from top-to-bottom and managed to find a restaurant. As is often the case in villages, the owner didn't quite understand the concept of vegetarianism and offered me cod, fried fish and calamari before finally settling on a giant piece of tortilla and a salad. The beast was temporarily sated. After charging it on a faulty powerpoint, I misread an 8% as 80% on my powerbank and continued. 

Despite the poor nutrition and the hunt for food, this turned out to be the longest day of the trip so far: 145km. On the hunt once again as the sun set, I searched another village for any sign of food. After begging in a bar, they sold me a loaf of bread. Out of options, I bought a pile of doughnuts and chips at the local servo. That night I ate chip sandwiches and passed out in a pristine abandoned rail station - too far away from civilisation to be sullied. It had been a long day.

The next day was shorter. Only 95km. But infinitely harder. It started well. It was Monday and I was waiting eagerly outside as the supermarkets sprung into action at 10am. From then on, my empty stomach growling as I roamed the aisles, I resolved always to carry an emergency box of pasta. After a coffee stop, my bags and powerbank were fully charged. Tired from the previous day, I had a beer and a lunchtime nap. Eye-of-the-tiger beast mode was looking more house-cat by the hour. 

I slowly left the orange, pomegranate and mandarin territory of Valencia and entered true olive territory and a hive of activity. Range rovers and tractors raced along the rural roads, crammed full of migrant workers. My first taste of Africa. It was harvest season and they were laying tarps around the trees and attaching a strange, loud machine to their trunks. Its entire job was to vibrate the tree, causing the olives to fall down onto the tarps. {{awkward}}

With the waiting and the napping, I hadn't covered much ground that day, so I decided to push on into the evening, making headway on the final pass before reaching Granada. With the sun setting, I realised the surroundings were slowly getting more rural and the surface reflected that, going from clean tarmac to gravel to single track. It started to rain. 

Then I hit mud. {{hyperbole. funny}} The clay stuck to BoB's wheels, jamming them. I cleaned them out, grabbing handfuls of mud from between the frame and wheels. After two metres of riding, it jammed again. I decided to walk instead. It still jammed. My feet started to slip as well. 

After 20 minutes of this game, I was exhausted. It was raining. There was nowhere to pitch my tent in this field of mud. I'd gone too far to turn back. The only option was to continue. Only 200m in front of me, at the top of a gentle slope, was a farmhouse. That was my target. 

Rolling wasn't going anywhere, so I gathered my strength and slung all 40kg of BoB over my shoulder and started to walk. It was hard going, but I was finally making progress. When planning the dramatic gesture, I'd forgotten my feet. With the extra weight, I slipped and fell hard, covering one side of my body in mud. After another 20 minutes of struggle I reached the top. The sun had well and truly set by this point. 

There was nobody at the farmhouse, which was in fact a stable, filled with sheep and goats {{true?}}. Some sheep dogs stood watch and started barking, which set of the other animals. I skidded my way to some nearby trees and began to set up my tent. The dogs didn't stop. I figured they'd eventually calm down. I didn't have the strength to continue. 

As I was putting up the tent, the farmer arrived with a flashlight, sliding up to the house in his ute {{really?}}. As he opened the gate, the dogs bolted out, straight to me. Luckily they weren't guard dogs, so they didn't bite. But they stood about a metre away from me, barking loudly. The game was up. I started to pack up my things. 

Something about the movement of this torch in the distance didn't seem inviting. I probably wouldn't be either if I'd been forced to risk my life driving on this pile of mud. In retrospect, I should have tried to talk to him. But I was too tired to talk. I picked up BoB and slid on.

The farmer's village was only one kilometre away, the lights clear, distinct and tantalising. Another half an hour extreme exertion followed. This time in the dark. Slipping and sliding and falling with 40kg on my shoulder {{joke about BoB losing weight}}. In the outskirts of the town, there was a vaguely flat patch of grass amidst the sea of clay. I set up my tent. 

Too tired to search for water, I invented the sock bath, wiping the mud off as best I could with a wet sock. I heard another car passing by, terrified {{too strong!}} that I'd have to pack up camp once again. They passed by, sliding into the night. Tired beyond words, I drifted off to sleep. 

The next morning I woke up crusted in mud. So was BoB. The priority was a good clean. I rolled through the village, which was deserted, as usual. Too early. I had to find another source of water. I rolled downhill and found a small stream under a bridge. 

I got stuck in cleaning the mud off BoB with a toothbrush. I managed to clean myself under the bridge as well, naked and covered in soap as passing trucks shook the bridge overhead. After four hours, we were both squeaky clean.
